The business case in three sentences

  1. The demand already exists. Hispanic shoppers spend more on beauty than their share of households, and they shop independent Latino grocery five times more often than the general population. Yet the beauty shelf in those stores is usually empty or unchanged in twenty years.
  2. We have something no one else has. A delivery route already entering that store every week, DCs nearby, and twenty years of trust. A new entrant would need three years to build what we already own.
  3. The product itself is competitive. The recent wave of K-pop, Korean drama and film runs unusually strong with this consumer, and K-Beauty can convert that interest with real functional performance. Our focus is supplying competitive product that turns that demand into sales.
State this up front, internally and to the retailer

We found no evidence of K-Beauty selling in US Hispanic grocery. No success cases, no failure cases. In fact the most recent study (Belleza Latina) puts Latina beauty shoppers in Sephora, Ulta and Amazon.

The cultural momentum is proven and the spending power is proven — but that these two meet in this channel is not yet proven. That is precisely why we run a 9-store, 90-day pilot. Being straight about this earns more credibility than overselling.

1-3. Functional competitiveness — claim only these three

Culture creates interest, but without product performance there is no reorder. However, our functional claims must be limited to three things. Anything beyond that is publicly contradicted by dermatologists and hands a buyer an easy rebuttal.

Safe to claim — sourced

1. Development speed

Korean ODMs go from concept to shelf in 6–8 months; large Western companies take 2–3 years. With roughly 30,000 registered cosmetics companies in Korea, competition forces the pace. Trends reach the shelf faster.

2. Sunscreen UV filters

The US has effectively one adequate UVA chemical filter — avobenzone. The FDA has not approved a new UV filter since 1999. Korea and Europe can use newer photostable filters such as Tinosorb S and Uvinul A Plus. A real technical advantage created by a regulatory gap.

3. Price-to-performance

Comparable function at a lower price point than Western brands. This is the argument that lands hardest in this channel.

Do not claim — you will be corrected

"Korean ingredients work better"

Dr. Shari Lipner (Weill Cornell): Western staples like retinol, vitamin C and peptides are "well studied and evidence-backed," whereas fermented ingredients and snail mucin "may help, but we know much less. The data isn't there yet."

Dr. Clare O'Connor: K-Beauty sunscreens are "not superior — at best equivalent" to European products.

Fast regulatory approval cuts both ways — it also means products can launch without a strong evidence base. Claim ingredient superiority and these quotes become the rebuttal.

Sources: NextPangaea (2026-05-06) · TODAY (2025-12-11) · BBC Science Focus (2026-06-07)

The weakest link — know it before someone finds it

No public data directly measures how much K-Beauty US Hispanic consumers actually buy. Hispanic Korean-content fandom (46%) and Hispanic beauty over-indexing (14.4% of households / 16.6% of dollars) are each proven, but no study crosses them. The NielsenIQ Hispanic beauty report does not mention K-Beauty at all. This is a well-founded hypothesis, not a proven fact — and it is exactly what the pilot is for.

An honest contradiction

Clinical visit data (NAMCS) does not place pigmentation in the top five diagnoses for Hispanic patients. Marketing research and clinical data disagree. That said, only 23.9% of Hispanic dermatologic visits reach an actual dermatologist, and Hispanic communities have the highest uninsured rate in the US — so clinic data likely undercounts cosmetic pigment concerns. This is unresolved. Say "the largest commercial opportunity," not "the #1 concern."

Favorable to an unknown brand

In Belleza Latina, 85% describe themselves as "beauty brand explorers." US Hispanic shoppers use 6.1 store banners on average vs 4.9 for the general market. And 80% want brands that genuinely understand the needs of people of color, while only 35% think the industry delivers — a 45-point gap. Loyalty here is earned, not default, which favors a new entrant and disadvantages incumbents.

04What the Incubation Strategy Changes

Not using famous brands is an advantage, not a weakness

What we gain — significant

The online price-collapse risk disappears

The biggest problem with famous K-Beauty brands is that the brands themselves sell at roughly half price on their own Amazon storefronts. Observed gaps: Biodance ~65–70% below its own DTC site, Medicube 52–55%, TIRTIR 46%, COSRX 53%.

Put that on a shelf and one phone search kills it. Sourced unknown product has no structural exposure to this — we are the only US route to market.

What we give up — plan for it

Zero brand recognition

We cannot open with "you know COSRX, right?" Neither the retailer nor the shopper knows these brands.

So the pitch shifts from brand to category, concern, price and delivery. Fortunately the cultural momentum in §1 works at the category level, not the brand level — in the Numerator study, what fans became interested in was "K-Beauty," not a specific brand.

Which is why the portfolio is built this way

The 14 SKUs below are defined by product type, consumer concern and price point — not by brand. When sourcing finds product that fits a slot, it drops in. If the brand changes, the planogram, the sales script and the Spanish shelf cards all still work.

ODM comes after the pilot tells us which types actually sell. Committing to ODM minimums before knowing what to make is the most expensive mistake available here.

Context — this is already happening

The ANUA × KPop Demon Hunters collaboration launched online in February 2026 and went into roughly 1,500 Ulta stores nationwide. Collagen masks at $4 each ($15 for four), spot patches $14 for 55 count, and limited bundles with collectible photocards — a K-pop fandom mechanic transplanted directly onto a beauty shelf.

H Mart has opened beauty sections branded "H Art" at select stores. Their stated rationale: "Korean food and skincare are all bundled together because both are essential in the day-to-day." That is the closest structural precedent for a food distributor adding skincare to a culturally-defined grocery channel — though H Mart put Korean product into Korean grocery, an ethnicity match our case does not have.

Basis and limits of the pricing

Two inputs. (1) Existing HBA shelf pricing in Hispanic grocery was observed at $3–$8 retail — from one retailer and one wholesaler, not a channel census. (2) But US Hispanic consumers spend 19% more on beauty than average.

So we anchor the impulse tier firmly at $1.99–$5.99 and take only the serums to $9.99. These are sourcing targets, not confirmed prices — final retail follows landed cost and retailer margin work.

Three placement rules
  1. Price rises from bottom to top. The $1.99 trial happens at the shelf her hand reaches first; satisfied shoppers move up. Arranging by routine order (cleanse → tone → treat) does not work in this channel.
  2. $1.99 and $4.99 must be legible from across the aisle. Small price tags make this read as an expensive set and nobody approaches it.
  3. Put acne patches in a separate checkout basket. It is the surest first sale here and the fastest way to prove "this moves" to the retailer.
Bilingual signage — there is data

A 2023 experimental study (two experiments, n=240 and n=236) found bilingual signage beat both English-only and Spanish-only. Behavioral intent: bilingual 4.8 > English 4.5 > Spanish-only 4.1 (bilingual vs Spanish-only, p=.01). Bilingual signage also significantly raised perceived authenticity (5.06 vs 4.45), inclusiveness (5.01 vs 4.40), warmth and interest.

And roughly 80% of US Latinos are bilingual or English-dominant. Spanish-only is not the answer.

Source: Mantel & Kellaris, IJSW 7(1), 2023 · Note: the sample was general US adults, not Hispanic-only. That arguably strengthens the commercial case — bilingual signage did not cost general-market intent, it raised it.

11Legal Red Lines

Every rep must read this section

Skin-lightening language is off-limits in the US

Since September 23, 2020, under FD&C Act §505G as amended by the CARES Act, OTC skin-lightening products containing hydroquinone are unapproved new drugs and misbranded and cannot be legally marketed in the US. The only FDA-approved hydroquinone product is the prescription drug Tri-Luma.

Enforcement is real. FDA issued 12 warning letters on April 19, 2022. In one of them, the label language FDA cited was:

  • "BLEACHING LOTION … Skin Lightener"
  • "For Bleaching of Hyperpigmented Skin"
  • References to chloasma, melasma, freckles and other conditions

The test is not the ingredient — it is "intended use," which FDA infers from the label as a whole. A single phrase can turn a cosmetic into a drug.
